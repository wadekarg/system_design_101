<Callout type="analogy">
Imagine a popular book that everyone in the world wants to read, but there's
only **one copy** in a library in London. A reader in Sydney has to wait for it
to be shipped halfway around the planet.

A **CDN** is like printing copies of that book and stocking them in a local
library in *every* major city. Now the Sydney reader walks down the street
instead of waiting on international post. The content didn't get faster to
produce — you just moved it *closer*.
</Callout>

## What a CDN is

A **Content Delivery Network (CDN)** is a network of servers spread across the
globe — called **Points of Presence (POPs)** or **edge nodes** — that keep
cached copies of your content near your users. Instead of every request flying
all the way to your **origin server** (maybe a single data center in Virginia),
users get served from a POP that might be in their own city.

The reason is pure physics. Light travels through fiber at about 200,000 km/s,
so a request from Sydney to London — roughly 16,000 km of cable — adds at least
**160 ms** of round-trip latency *before any server does work*. A POP in Sydney
answers the same user in **5–10 ms**. For images, scripts, fonts, and video,
that gap is the dominant factor in how fast a page *feels*.

<Callout type="tip">
Major CDNs like **Cloudflare** and **Akamai** run *hundreds* of POPs across
dozens of countries and collectively carry a large fraction of all internet
traffic. That sheer scale is also what lets them soak up attacks — Cloudflare
has absorbed DDoS floods exceeding **1 terabit per second**.
</Callout>

## How a CDN works: hit vs miss

Every request to a CDN comes down to one question: *do I already have a valid
copy of this?*

- **Cache hit** — the POP has a fresh copy. It serves the response instantly
  from local memory or disk, never touching your origin.
- **Cache miss** — the POP has nothing (or a stale copy). It forwards the
  request to your origin, caches whatever comes back, and returns it. Every
  later request near that POP is now a hit.

| Situation | What happens | Latency | Origin load |
|---|---|---|---|
| **Cache hit** | Served from the edge | Very low (5–10 ms) | None |
| **Cache miss** | Fetched from origin, then cached | High (full round-trip) | One hit |
| **Stale but revalidated** | 304 from origin, cached copy served | Medium | Light |

How long a POP holds a copy is decided by the **Cache-Control** and **Expires**
headers your origin sends. A content-addressed bundle (`app.a3f92c.js`) can be
cached forever; a daily HTML page maybe an hour; a user's private account data,
not at all.

<Callout type="tip">
Cache keys default to the **full URL, query string included**.
`/products?category=shoes` and `/products?category=hats` are separate entries.
That's also why a stray `?utm_source=email` tanks your hit rate — it forges a
brand-new key. Most CDNs let you strip such parameters from the key.
</Callout>

## Push vs Pull CDN

There are two ways to get content into the edge caches, and they trade
simplicity against control.

| | **Pull CDN** | **Push CDN** |
|---|---|---|
| Who populates it | The CDN, lazily on first request | You, explicitly, ahead of time |
| First request | Cache miss → hits origin | Already cached → hit |
| Deploy work | None — new content picked up automatically | You push assets in your pipeline |
| Invalidation | Content expires by TTL | You manage manually |
| Best for | Most websites and APIs | Predictable, heavy launches (movie premieres) |

A **pull CDN** is the common, lazy model: the first request for a resource at a
POP misses and fetches from origin; everything after is a hit. You do nothing
special on deploy — the CDN just picks up new content as people ask for it.

A **push CDN** makes you upload content ahead of time. More operational work,
but no cold first-request penalty — ideal when you *know* a surge is coming, like
a streaming service pre-positioning a movie across every POP before an 8pm
premiere.

## Cache-Control headers

The `Cache-Control` response header (sent by your origin) is how you steer
caching. The directives that matter most:

- **`max-age=N`** — cache for N seconds, for browsers *and* CDNs.
- **`s-maxage=N`** — same, but for **shared caches (CDNs) only**; overrides
  `max-age` at the edge. Lets you say "CDN, hold this a day; browser, ten minutes."
- **`no-cache`** — *not* "don't cache." It means "cache it, but **revalidate**
  with the origin before each serve" (via `If-None-Match` / `If-Modified-Since`;
  a 304 means the cached copy is still good).
- **`no-store`** — truly never cache.
- **`public`** — any cache may store it, CDNs included.
- **`private`** — only the **browser** may cache it, never a shared CDN — the
  right setting for user-specific responses.

<Callout type="tip">
The frontend gold standard: ship static assets with content-addressed filenames
and `Cache-Control: public, max-age=31536000, immutable`. Browser and CDN cache
them for a year; the *only* way to update is to change the filename — which Vite
and Webpack do automatically when content changes.
</Callout>

## Where this shows up

- **Static assets (images, JS bundles, fonts, video)** → the ideal CDN case:
  stable content, large payloads (origin round-trip is expensive), and high
  request frequency, so hit rates soar.
- **Video streaming (Netflix, YouTube)** → push-heavy; popular titles are
  pre-positioned at the edge so millions stream without touching origin.
- **Global SaaS dashboards** → cache the static shell aggressively, mark
  personalized API responses `private` so per-user data never gets shared.
- **News / high-traffic sites during spikes** → the CDN absorbs a flash crowd
  (a story going viral) that would otherwise flatten the origin.
- **Any HTTPS site** → **TLS termination** at the edge cuts the 1–2 handshake
  round-trips, since the handshake happens at a nearby POP, not across the world.

## Pressure points

<Callout type="warning">
The classic CDN trap is **stale content**. You cache aggressively with a long
TTL, then push a fix — and users keep seeing the old version until the TTL
expires, because the CDN still thinks its copy is valid.

**Cache invalidation is hard.** Manual purges are slow to propagate across
hundreds of POPs and easy to forget. And beware caching too *little*: mark a
page `private` or `no-store` by mistake and every request stampedes your origin,
erasing the whole point of the CDN.
</Callout>

## Handling the pressure points

- **Version your URLs.** Content-addressed filenames (`app.a3f92c.js`) make every
  deploy a new cache key, so updates are instant and no purge is needed.
- **Split TTLs by volatility.** `immutable` for hashed assets; short TTLs for HTML;
  `no-store` only for truly private data. Don't paint everything with one brush.
- **Use `s-maxage` + revalidation.** Long edge TTL for reach, `no-cache`
  revalidation where freshness matters, so a 304 keeps it cheap.
- **Tune the cache key.** Strip tracking params (`utm_*`) and irrelevant query
  strings so equivalent requests share one entry and hit rates climb.
- **Purge as a backstop, not a habit.** Keep the purge API for emergencies, but
  design so you rarely need it.

## Alternatives & related ideas

<Callout type="alternative">
A CDN caches at the *network edge*, but it's one layer in a bigger caching
stack. **Browser caching** keeps assets on the user's own machine (zero network
at all). **Reverse-proxy / origin caches** (Varnish, nginx) sit in front of your
app for hits that never even reach application code. **Application caches**
(Redis, Memcached) hold computed results and query output.

For **DDoS and security** specifically, CDNs bundle a **WAF** and edge filtering,
but dedicated scrubbing services exist too. And for truly dynamic, personalized
responses that can't be cached, **edge compute** (Cloudflare Workers,
Lambda@Edge) runs your logic *at* the POP instead of just caching bytes.
</Callout>

## Summary

A CDN moves copies of your content to **POPs near your users**, turning
continent-spanning round-trips into local ones — the single biggest lever on
perceived load speed. Requests are **hits** (served instantly at the edge) or
**misses** (fetched from origin and then cached), and you steer that behavior
with **Cache-Control** headers: `max-age`/`s-maxage` for TTLs, `private` for
user data, `immutable` for hashed assets. Choose a **pull CDN** for almost
everything and a **push CDN** when you must pre-warm for a known surge. The hard
part is always **cache invalidation** — solve it with versioned URLs rather than
manual purges — and remember the CDN gives you DDoS protection, TLS termination,
and a WAF almost for free.

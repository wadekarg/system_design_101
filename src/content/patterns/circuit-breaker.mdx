<Callout type="analogy">
Think of the **circuit breaker in your home's fuse box**. When a short causes
current to spike, the breaker *snaps open* and cuts the circuit — instantly.
It doesn't sit there letting the wire heat up until your house catches fire.

A software circuit breaker does the same for a **failing dependency**. When a
downstream service starts misbehaving, the breaker trips and stops sending it
calls, protecting both the caller and the sick service. Later it *carefully*
tests whether the danger has passed before flipping back on.
</Callout>

## The problem: cascading failures

In a microservice system, services lean on each other. **Service A** calls
**B**, which calls **C**. When everything is healthy, requests flow. But watch
what happens when C gets slow — a bad query, a memory leak, plain overload.

B waits on C's slow responses, holding threads and connections open. As B's
thread pool fills with requests stuck waiting on C, *new* requests to B start
queuing too. B slows down. Now A is waiting on B, A's pool fills, and A starts
failing. One deep service degraded — and the whole chain fell over. That's a
**cascading failure**.

<Callout type="warning">
The core insight: **slow is worse than down.** If C were simply *offline*, B
would fail instantly and free its threads. A *slow* C holds threads hostage
while doing no useful work. And the more traffic you push, the faster the
collapse spreads. This is one of the most common causes of large-scale outages.
</Callout>

## The circuit breaker pattern

A circuit breaker **wraps every call** to a downstream dependency with logic
that can halt requests when that dependency is failing. Your code calls
*through* the breaker, never directly to the service.

Its signature move is **fail fast**: instead of waiting on a service you already
know is unhealthy, reject the call immediately with an error. This does three
good things at once:

- **Frees threads** in the caller (no more waiting on doomed calls)
- **Cuts latency** — callers get an instant error, not a 30-second timeout
- **Gives the sick service room to breathe** instead of bombarding it

The breaker keeps its own **state**, records the outcome of each call
(success or failure), and decides whether to let calls through or reject them.

## The three states

The breaker is a **finite state machine** with three states. This table is the
whole pattern in one glance:

| State | What it does | When it changes |
|---|---|---|
| **Closed** (normal) | Requests pass through; count successes/failures | Failure rate crosses threshold (e.g. >50% in 10s) → **Open** |
| **Open** (tripped) | Reject *all* calls instantly, no attempt made | After a timer (typically 30–60s) → **Half-Open** |
| **Half-Open** (probing) | Let a *small* number of probe calls through | Probe succeeds → **Closed** · Probe fails → back to **Open** |

**Closed** is business as usual — traffic flows and the breaker just watches. If
too many calls fail within the window, it **trips** to Open.

**Open** is the tripped state. Every call is rejected in *microseconds* instead
of hanging on a timeout. This instantly relieves pressure on both sides. A timer
runs, and when it expires the breaker moves to Half-Open.

**Half-Open** is the cautious test. The breaker lets *one* (or a few) probe
requests through. Probe succeeds? The service has likely recovered — reset the
counters and go **Closed**. Probe fails? Still sick — snap back to **Open** and
restart the timer.

<Callout type="tip">
This three-state loop is **automatic recovery** with no human in the loop. The
system self-heals as the dependency comes back, and the single-probe design
tests recovery *without* flooding a fragile service and knocking it over again.
</Callout>

## Thresholds and fallbacks

A breaker is only as good as its config. **Too sensitive** and it trips on brief
blips; **too lenient** and it lets a cascade run wild. The knobs that matter:

- **Minimum calls** in the window before it can trip (don't trip on the very first failure)
- A **failure rate** threshold — a percentage, not an absolute count
- The **time window** for measuring failures
- The **Open duration** before probing again
- The **number of probes** allowed in Half-Open

Just as important as the breaker is the **fallback** — what you return when the
circuit is Open. Handing the user a raw error is rarely the right call. Better:

- Serve a **cached** last-good response (stale data often beats no data)
- Return a **default or empty** response ("recommendations unavailable" beats a dead page)
- Pull from an **alternative, less authoritative** source
- **Queue** the operation to run later when the service recovers

<Callout type="tip">
Design the fallback **up front**, not as an afterthought. And for a critical
dependency with no meaningful fallback, failing fast with a clear error still
beats a timeout — it preserves the caller's thread and gives fast feedback.
</Callout>

## Where this shows up

- **Netflix** pioneered the pattern at scale with **Hystrix**, wrapping every
  cross-service call so one slow backend couldn't take down streaming.
- **E-commerce product pages** wrap non-critical widgets (recommendations,
  reviews) so a sick widget service degrades gracefully instead of failing checkout.
- **Payment gateways** wrap third-party processors — if one processor gets slow,
  fail fast and route to a backup rather than hanging the checkout thread.
- **Service meshes (Istio, Linkerd)** apply circuit breaking in the sidecar
  proxy, so *every* service gets it uniformly with zero application code.
- **Mobile/API backends** wrap fan-out calls so one degraded microservice
  doesn't blow the latency budget for the whole response.

## Handling the pressure points

- **Spurious trips on brief spikes?** → require a minimum call count and trip on
  a *rate*, not an absolute count, so a 1-second blip doesn't flip the breaker.
- **Cascade already spreading?** → make sure calls actually go *through* the
  breaker, and pair it with **timeouts** so slow calls count as failures fast.
- **Retry storms?** → don't retry blindly against an Open breaker; add
  **exponential backoff + jitter** so recovering services aren't re-flooded.
- **One service exhausting everything?** → add **bulkheads** (below) to cap the
  blast radius per dependency.

<Callout type="warning">
The nastiest failure mode is the **retry storm**: a service wobbles, every
caller retries at once, and the synchronized flood finishes the job the original
glitch started. A breaker plus **backoff with jitter** spreads retries out and
stops callers from turning a hiccup into an outage.
</Callout>

## Alternatives & related ideas

<Callout type="alternative">
Circuit breakers rarely work alone — they're one layer of a resilience stack:

- **Timeouts** are the foundation. Without an aggressive per-call timeout, a slow
  dependency hangs your thread indefinitely and the breaker never even sees a
  "failure" to count. Always set them.
- **Retries + exponential backoff (with jitter)** handle *transient* blips a
  breaker shouldn't trip on — but naive retries cause storms, so pair them with a
  breaker that stops retrying once a dependency is clearly down.
- **Bulkheads** give **spatial** isolation (separate thread pools per dependency)
  where the breaker gives **temporal** isolation (trip and recover). Together
  they bound the blast radius of any single failure.
</Callout>

The **bulkhead pattern** — named for the watertight compartments in a ship's
hull — gives each downstream dependency its *own* thread pool. If Service C goes
slow and ties up its 10 dedicated threads, new C calls get rejected immediately,
but calls to A and B (with separate pools) sail on untouched. Without bulkheads,
one slow service can drain your *entire* application thread pool and fail
everything. Combine bulkheads' isolation with the breaker's trip-and-recover
intelligence and you have the backbone of a resilient microservice architecture.

Established libraries handle the tricky concurrency and timing: **Resilience4j**
(Java, the modern JVM standard), **Hystrix** (Java, Netflix's original, now in
maintenance), and **Polly** (.NET) all bundle breakers, retries, timeouts, and
bulkheads together.

## Summary

A circuit breaker **fails fast** when a dependency is unhealthy, so one slow
service can't exhaust threads upstream and cascade into a full outage — because
**slow is worse than down**. It runs as a three-state machine: **Closed** passes
traffic and watches, **Open** rejects instantly to relieve pressure, and
**Half-Open** sends a careful probe to test recovery before flipping back. Tune
it with a minimum call count and a failure *rate* to avoid spurious trips, and
always design a **fallback** (cache, default, or queue) for when it's Open. Pair
it with **timeouts**, **backoff-with-jitter retries**, and **bulkheads** to turn
a fragile call chain into one that bends instead of breaking.

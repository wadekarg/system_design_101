<Callout type="analogy">
Booking a trip means locking in a **flight**, a **hotel**, and a **rental car** — and they only make sense together. If the flight books but the hotel is full, you don't want to fly to a city with nowhere to sleep. Either the whole trip comes together, or you cancel the parts that went through.

The catch: three different companies, three different systems. No single "book my trip" button spans all of them. You have to coordinate them yourself — and decide what to do when step two fails after step one already charged your card. That's the entire problem of **distributed transactions**.
</Callout>

## The problem: no single ACID transaction to save you

Inside **one** database, a transaction gives you **ACID** guarantees — most importantly **Atomicity**: all operations succeed or none do. Debit $100 from account A and credit $100 to account B, and the database ensures both happen or neither does. Money can't vanish mid-transfer.

In a **microservice** architecture, that safety net disappears. An e-commerce checkout might:

- Charge the card in the **Payment Service**
- Deduct stock in the **Inventory Service**
- Create the record in the **Order Service**
- Send a receipt in the **Notification Service**

Each service has its own database. **No single transaction spans all four.** So what happens when payment succeeds but inventory fails? You've charged a customer for something you can't ship. The challenge is getting atomicity — *all steps, or a clean recoverable state* — across independent systems.

## Two-phase commit (2PC)

**2PC** is the classic protocol for atomic commitment across multiple participants. A central **coordinator** drives everyone through two phases.

- **Phase 1 — Prepare:** the coordinator asks every participant "can you commit?" Each does the work up to the commit point (grabs locks, writes to a temp area) and votes `COMMIT` or `ABORT`. A yes vote is a durable promise: "I can still commit even if you tell me much later."
- **Phase 2 — Commit or Abort:** if *everyone* voted commit, the coordinator tells all to `COMMIT`. If anyone voted abort or went silent, it tells all to `ABORT` and they roll back.

This gives **strong atomicity** — if the coordinator says commit, everyone commits. It's how **XA transactions** work in MySQL, PostgreSQL, and Oracle for cross-database operations.

<Callout type="tip">
2PC shines when you own both databases and they speak XA — say, two Postgres instances behind one service. It falls apart the moment you reach across service boundaries or call a third-party API that can't join your commit protocol.
</Callout>

The fatal flaw is **blocking**. If the coordinator crashes *after* Prepare but *before* Commit, every participant that voted yes is stuck. It can't abort (it promised to commit) and can't commit (it doesn't know the final decision). It sits there **holding locks** until the coordinator recovers — and while it waits, other transactions queue behind those locks. Under load, this cascades into severe degradation.

## Three-phase commit (3PC)

**3PC** tries to fix blocking by inserting a **pre-commit** phase between Prepare and Commit. Once a participant sees `PRE-COMMIT`, it knows *everyone* voted yes — so if the coordinator then dies, it can safely commit on its own after a timeout. No more deadlock.

But 3PC assumes a **synchronous network** and breaks under **partitions**. If the network splits, participants on different sides can make conflicting decisions — some commit, some abort. Since partitions are a fact of life, 3PC is rarely used in production. Most systems reach for Sagas instead.

## Saga pattern

A **Saga** gives up on global atomicity. Instead it's a **sequence of local transactions**, each a normal ACID commit in one service — and each paired with a **compensating transaction** that undoes it. If any step fails, the saga runs the compensations in reverse to unwind what already happened.

For the checkout, the saga might be:

1. `CreateOrder` → produces an order ID
2. `ReserveInventory` → reduces available stock
3. `ProcessPayment` → charges the customer
4. `ConfirmOrder` → marks the order confirmed
5. `SendConfirmation` → emails the receipt

If step 3 fails, the compensations are `ReleaseInventory` (restore stock) and `CancelOrder` (mark it cancelled).

Compensations must be **idempotent** (safe to run more than once, since the saga may retry them) and must accept that **the world moved on**. You can't un-send an email — so the "compensation" for a sent notification is to send a *cancellation* email instead.

<Callout type="tip">
Sagas come in two flavors. **Choreography**: each service listens for events and reacts, with no central brain — simple, but the workflow is scattered across services and hard to trace. **Orchestration**: a central orchestrator issues commands and tracks progress — one place to see, debug, and visualize the flow. Reach for orchestration once a saga has more than a couple of steps.
</Callout>

## 2PC vs Saga

| | Two-phase commit (2PC) | Saga |
|---|---|---|
| **Consistency** | Strong — atomic, no visible intermediate states | Eventual — brief in-between states are visible |
| **Failure recovery** | Coordinator replays the decision | Compensating transactions undo completed steps |
| **Coupling** | Tight — all participants join one lock protocol | Loose — each service commits independently |
| **Blocking** | Yes — locks held until coordinator resolves | No — no distributed locks held across steps |
| **Works across services / 3rd-party APIs?** | Poorly (needs XA) | Yes — the standard microservices choice |
| **Best for** | Two databases you own, both XA-capable | Microservices, long-running business workflows |

## Where this shows up

- **E-commerce checkout** → **Saga**. Order, inventory, payment, and notifications live in separate services; brief "pending" states are fine, and a failed payment triggers compensations.
- **Banking core ledgers (single DB cluster)** → **2PC / XA**. Two databases you fully control, where a customer must *never* see money in a partial state.
- **Travel / trip booking** → **Saga**. Flight, hotel, and car are external providers you can't lock — you book each, and cancel the ones that went through if a later step fails.
- **User onboarding** (create account → provision workspace → send welcome) → **Saga**, usually **choreographed** via events since the steps are loosely related.
- **Payment gateways** (Stripe, PayPal) → **idempotency keys everywhere**, because clients retry aggressively and double-charging is unacceptable.

<Callout type="warning">
The traps to plan for:

- **2PC blocking / coordinator failure** — a dead coordinator freezes locked resources. *Mitigation:* keep 2PC to databases you own, add coordinator timeouts and recovery logs, or avoid it across services entirely.
- **Saga compensation complexity** — you must design a correct "undo" for every step, including irreversible ones. *Mitigation:* make compensations idempotent, model irreversible actions as *forward* recovery (send a cancellation, issue a refund), and test failure at each step.
- **No isolation** — sagas expose intermediate states, so another request can read a half-finished order. *Mitigation:* use semantic locks / status flags (`PENDING`, `CONFIRMED`), and design reads to ignore or hide in-flight rows.
</Callout>

## Handling the pressure points

- **Retries are inevitable** → make every operation **idempotent**, so a repeated request produces the same result as one.
- **Coordinator is a single point of failure** → replicate it, persist its decision log, and give participants safe timeout behavior.
- **Compensations can fail too** → retry them with backoff, and alert a human when a saga is stuck mid-unwind.
- **Debugging distributed flows is hard** → prefer **orchestration** for complex sagas so the whole workflow lives in one auditable place.

## Idempotency

**Idempotency** means applying an operation many times has the same effect as applying it once. In distributed systems it's not optional — it's the foundation of correctness.

Networks drop *responses*, not just requests. The client sends a charge, the reply is lost, the client retries — but the server already charged the card. Without idempotency, that's a **double charge**.

The fix is an **idempotency key**: a unique ID the client attaches to the request (e.g. `Idempotency-Key: <uuid>`). The server records the key and its outcome the first time. If the same key returns, it replays the stored result instead of re-executing. Keys typically expire after a TTL like 24 hours.

<Callout type="tip">
The check-and-store must be **atomic**, or two concurrent retries can both find the key missing and both execute. A Redis `SET NX`, a database unique constraint, or a compare-and-swap gives you that atomicity in one step.
</Callout>

Idempotency keys are what make saga compensations safe to retry and at-least-once message delivery safe to act on. They're the mechanism that lets a distributed system tolerate unreliable networks without corrupting data.

## Alternatives & related ideas

<Callout type="alternative">
When you can't (or shouldn't) coordinate synchronously, lean on these building blocks:

- **Transactional outbox** — write your business change *and* an "event to publish" in the *same* local transaction, then a relay ships the event. This closes the gap where a commit succeeds but the follow-up message is lost.
- **Idempotency** — the safety net under retries, at-least-once delivery, and compensations. Design for it everywhere.
- **Eventual consistency** — accept brief inconsistency in exchange for availability and loose coupling. Most business operations (orders, signups, payments) tolerate a "pending" moment just fine.

The goal isn't to fake single-database atomicity across services — that road leads to blocking and tight coupling. It's to design operations that *tolerate* distributed failure.
</Callout>

## Summary

Inside one database, ACID hands you atomicity for free; across services, you build it yourself. **2PC** delivers strong consistency but **blocks** when the coordinator fails and needs XA support that microservices lack. **3PC** eases blocking but breaks under network partitions, so it stays on the shelf. In practice, **Sagas** — local transactions plus **compensating actions** — win for microservices, trading strict consistency for **eventual consistency** and resilience. Underneath all of it, **idempotency keys** and the **transactional outbox** are what keep retries and lost messages from corrupting your data.

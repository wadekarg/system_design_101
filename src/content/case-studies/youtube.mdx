<Callout type="analogy">
Think of YouTube like a restaurant that also runs a warehouse. **Uploading** is
the delivery dock: a supplier drops off raw ingredients (your original video
file) that nobody can eat yet. **Transcoding** is the prep kitchen — it turns
those raw ingredients into ready-to-serve dishes in several portion sizes
(360p, 720p, 1080p, 4K), pre-plated into bite-sized pieces. **Streaming** is the
waiter delivering those bites to your table one at a time, swapping to a smaller
portion the instant your appetite (bandwidth) shrinks.

The two hard jobs are completely different: getting raw goods *in* reliably, and
serving finished dishes *out* to millions of tables at once. Design them
separately and the whole system gets simpler.
</Callout>

## Requirements

YouTube's product is complex — comments, recommendations, monetization — but the system design interview focuses on two flows: upload and stream. Scope the problem before designing.

**Functional requirements:**
- Creators can upload videos from a web browser or mobile app.
- Videos are available for playback after a processing delay (not instant).
- Viewers can stream videos at multiple quality levels (360p through 4K).
- The video quality adapts automatically based on the viewer's network speed.
- Basic search: find videos by title.

**Non-functional requirements:**
- **Scale**: 500 hours of video are uploaded to YouTube every minute. Over 1 billion hours of video are watched daily.
- **Upload reliability**: large uploads must survive network interruptions.
- **Streaming latency**: video playback should start within a few seconds and buffer minimally.
- **Storage cost**: storing and serving petabytes of video economically requires intelligent caching.
- **Durability**: uploaded videos must never be silently lost.

The upload and streaming flows have fundamentally different requirements — different infrastructure, different bottlenecks, different failure modes. Design them separately.

## Scale Estimation

**Upload throughput**: 500 hours of video per minute. Assuming average video is compressed to roughly 1 GB per hour of footage:

```
Upload bandwidth = 500 GB/min ≈ 8.3 GB/sec incoming
```

That is the raw ingestion bandwidth. After transcoding to multiple resolutions (360p, 720p, 1080p, 4K), storage multiplies — estimate 5x the raw size across all variants:

```
Storage growth ≈ 8.3 GB/sec × 5 = 41.5 GB/sec written to object storage
```

**Viewing scale**: 1 billion hours watched per day:

```
Concurrent viewers ≈ 1,000,000,000 hours / 24 = 41.7M simultaneous streams
```

Video bitrates range from 0.5 Mbps (360p) to 25 Mbps (4K). At an average of 2 Mbps per stream:

```
Outbound bandwidth ≈ 41.7M × 2 Mbps = 83 Tbps
```

No single data center handles 83 Tbps of outbound video. This number immediately tells you the answer: a global CDN is not optional — it is the only way the economics work.

<Callout type="warning">
The bottleneck to name out loud is **outbound bandwidth**, not storage. Storage
grows steadily and is cheap per GB; you can always buy more disks. But **83 Tbps
of egress** through one origin is physically and financially impossible — a
single 100 Gbps link would need ~830 of them just for the origin. The fix is to
push traffic to the *edge*: a CDN turns one 83 Tbps problem into thousands of
small, local ones, each served from a cache near the viewer.
</Callout>

<Callout type="tip">
In practice, do the two "shape" calculations first and let them drive the design.
The **8.3 GB/sec inbound** number tells you upload needs durable object storage
and resumable transfers; the **83 Tbps outbound** number single-handedly justifies
the CDN. Interviewers care less about your exact GB-per-hour guess than that you
noticed inbound and outbound are orders of magnitude apart.
</Callout>

## Video Upload Flow

Uploading a raw video file — often multiple gigabytes — over the internet requires a design that handles unreliable connections gracefully. A single HTTP PUT of a 10 GB file is impractical; any network interruption means starting over.

**Chunked (resumable) upload**:
1. The client sends a metadata request first: `POST /upload/init` with title, description, filesize. The server returns an `upload_id`.
2. The client splits the video into 4–8 MB chunks and uploads each chunk with its byte range: `PUT /upload/{upload_id}?offset=0`, `PUT /upload/{upload_id}?offset=8388608`, and so on.
3. Each chunk is acknowledged by the server. The client tracks which chunks are done.
4. If the connection drops, the client resumes from the last acknowledged chunk — no wasted work.
5. Once all chunks are received, the server assembles the file in object storage and enqueues it for processing.

The chunk size of 4–8 MB is a practical sweet spot: small enough that a failed chunk is cheap to retry, large enough that HTTP overhead per chunk is negligible.

The raw uploaded file lands in **object storage** (S3 or Google Cloud Storage) tagged as "raw/unprocessed." Object storage is durable (11 nines), infinitely scalable, and cheap per GB — exactly right for large binary blobs that are written once and read many times.

## Video Processing Pipeline

A raw video cannot be streamed directly to viewers for several reasons: it may be in any codec or container format, it has only one resolution, and it has not been segmented into the small chunks that adaptive streaming requires. Processing is mandatory before a video is available for playback.

The processing pipeline is **asynchronous**:

1. The upload service writes the raw file to object storage and publishes a message to a **message queue** (e.g., Kafka or SQS): `{"video_id": "abc123", "raw_path": "s3://raw/abc123.mp4"}`.
2. A pool of **transcoding workers** pulls jobs from the queue. Each worker:
   - Downloads the raw video from object storage.
   - Runs **FFmpeg** to transcode it into multiple output formats: 360p H.264, 720p H.264, 1080p H.264, 4K VP9 (Google's own codec for efficiency).
   - Splits each output into small fixed-duration segments (e.g., 2-second chunks) as required by HLS and DASH streaming protocols.
   - Generates a **manifest file** (`.m3u8` for HLS, `.mpd` for DASH) that lists all segments and their quality variants.
   - Uploads all segments and the manifest to object storage under a "processed" prefix.
3. When processing completes, the worker updates the video's status in the metadata database to "available" and publishes a completion event.

Transcoding is CPU-intensive: transcoding one hour of 1080p video can take 30+ minutes on a single CPU core. The message queue allows you to scale the worker pool horizontally — add more workers during peak hours, scale down overnight. Workers are stateless, so autoscaling is straightforward.

<Callout type="tip">
In practice, big videos are split so several workers transcode different
segments of the *same* video in parallel, then the pieces are stitched back
together. This turns a 30-minute serial job into a few minutes of wall-clock
time. It's also why the pipeline runs one codec per tier — cheap **H.264** for
360p/720p/1080p, and the more efficient **VP9/AV1** for 4K where the bandwidth
savings pay for the extra CPU.
</Callout>

The result of processing one uploaded video is many artifacts in object storage: dozens of segment files per quality level, manifest files, and auto-generated thumbnails (sampled at fixed intervals by a separate thumbnail worker).

## Video Streaming

When a viewer presses play, they do not receive the entire video file at once — that would waste bandwidth and memory. Instead, they receive a stream of small segments fetched on demand. This is called **Adaptive Bitrate (ABR) streaming**, and the two dominant protocols are **HLS** (HTTP Live Streaming, Apple) and **DASH** (Dynamic Adaptive Streaming over HTTP, MPEG).

The flow:
1. The player fetches the manifest file: `GET /videos/abc123/manifest.m3u8`.
2. The manifest lists all available quality variants and their segment URLs:
   ```
   #EXT-X-STREAM-INF:BANDWIDTH=800000,RESOLUTION=640x360
   /videos/abc123/360p/index.m3u8
   #EXT-X-STREAM-INF:BANDWIDTH=3000000,RESOLUTION=1280x720
   /videos/abc123/720p/index.m3u8
   #EXT-X-STREAM-INF:BANDWIDTH=8000000,RESOLUTION=1920x1080
   /videos/abc123/1080p/index.m3u8
   ```
3. The player's ABR algorithm measures current bandwidth and buffer health, then selects the appropriate quality tier.
4. The player fetches the next segment (e.g., `segment_003.ts`) before the current one finishes playing, maintaining a 30-second buffer.
5. If network conditions change (bandwidth drops, packet loss), the ABR algorithm switches to a lower quality tier on the next segment boundary — seamlessly, without pausing.

**Why segments?** Each 2-second segment is independently decodable. Switching quality tiers just means fetching the next segment from a different quality folder — no re-buffering required. The segment size balances between startup latency (smaller segments = faster first byte) and efficiency (larger segments = less HTTP overhead).

All segments and manifests are served through a CDN — the origin server (object storage) is rarely hit for popular videos.

<Callout type="tip">
In practice the whole ABR loop is client-side: **the player**, not the server,
decides which quality to fetch next based on measured bandwidth and buffer
health. That keeps the server dumb and stateless — it just hands out static
segment files over plain HTTP. Anything cacheable over plain HTTP is trivially
CDN-friendly, which is exactly why streaming was built this way.
</Callout>

## Metadata and Search

Video file bytes and video metadata have different access patterns and belong in different stores.

The **metadata database** (MySQL) stores:

```sql
CREATE TABLE videos (
  video_id     VARCHAR(20) PRIMARY KEY,   -- e.g. "dQw4w9WgXcQ"
  title        VARCHAR(200),
  description  TEXT,
  uploader_id  BIGINT NOT NULL,
  status       ENUM('processing', 'available', 'deleted'),
  duration_sec INT,
  view_count   BIGINT DEFAULT 0,
  like_count   BIGINT DEFAULT 0,
  created_at   TIMESTAMP,
  thumbnail_url TEXT
);
```

MySQL handles relational queries well: "get all videos by user X," "get video details for player page." View counts are a high-update field — avoid per-view SQL updates; instead, batch count increments and apply them periodically.

**Search** requires full-text indexing. MySQL's full-text search degrades at YouTube's scale. The standard solution is **Elasticsearch**: index video titles and descriptions, enabling fast keyword search with relevance ranking. When a video is created or updated, a sync process keeps Elasticsearch in sync with MySQL.

**Recommendations** are a separate ML system that falls outside this design. Acknowledge it exists and move on.

Thumbnails are static images served through the CDN — they are generated during the transcoding pipeline and stored in object storage alongside the video segments.

## CDN Strategy

The CDN is not a nice-to-have — it is what makes YouTube's bandwidth bill survivable. Without it, every video stream would originate from a central data center, and 83 Tbps of outbound traffic through a single origin is not achievable at any reasonable cost.

**How CDN caching works for video**: when a viewer in London watches a popular video, the CDN edge node in London fetches the video segments from the origin once and caches them locally. Every subsequent viewer in London (and the surrounding region) is served from the London edge node. The origin server sees only the first request.

**The 80/20 rule is critical here**: roughly 20% of videos account for 80% of views. The CDN's cache hit rate for popular videos approaches 100%. The long tail of rarely-watched videos (older uploads, niche content) stays at origin, but they account for only 20% of traffic. CDN economics work because of this distribution.

**Cache control headers**: popular videos use long `Cache-Control: max-age` headers (24 hours or more). The URL for each segment includes a content hash or version, so when a video is reprocessed (e.g., due to a quality bug), new segment URLs are generated and the CDN cache is bypassed automatically.

**Regional architecture**: YouTube operates multiple origin regions. A user in Asia uploads to the nearest upload endpoint; their video is stored in an Asian data center first, then replicated globally for CDN seeding. Regional origins reduce upload latency and mean the CDN in each region has a nearby origin to fill from.

The full data flow for a view: viewer's player fetches manifest from CDN edge → CDN edge serves cached manifest (or fetches from origin and caches) → player fetches 2-second segments from CDN edge → CDN edge serves from local cache with near-zero latency. The origin object storage sees only cache misses — typically the first viewer in each region for each video.

## Summary

YouTube is really two systems bolted to a shared storage layer: a reliable **upload** path and a massively-scaled **streaming** path. Uploads use **chunked, resumable transfers** into durable **object storage**, then an **asynchronous transcoding pipeline** (message queue plus stateless workers running FFmpeg) turns each raw file into multiple quality tiers pre-cut into small **HLS/DASH segments**. Playback uses **adaptive bitrate streaming**, where the client-side player picks a quality tier per segment based on live bandwidth — swapping down seamlessly when the network degrades. The dominant constraint is **outbound bandwidth** (~83 Tbps at peak), which is only survivable because a **global CDN** caches the ~20% of videos that drive ~80% of views close to viewers. Metadata and search live in separate stores (MySQL plus Elasticsearch) because video bytes and video facts have completely different access patterns.

<Callout type="analogy">
Think about a **24-hour convenience store**. **Availability** is whether the
doors are open when you show up. **Reliability** is whether the shelves actually
have what you came for — and whether the price at the register matches the tag.

A store can be open all night (available) but still fail you if the milk is spoiled
or the card reader charges you twice (unreliable). Great systems, like great stores,
have to be *both* open **and** trustworthy.
</Callout>

## Availability: the nines

**Availability** is the percentage of time a system is up and able to serve
requests. It is almost always written as a number of **nines**, and each extra
nine cuts your allowed downtime by **10x**.

The percentages look tiny on paper. Translated into real time, the gaps are huge:

| Availability | Nines | Downtime / year | Downtime / month |
|---|---|---|---|
| 99% | two nines | 3.65 days | 7.2 hours |
| 99.9% | three nines | 8.76 hours | 43 minutes |
| 99.99% | four nines | 52 minutes | 4.3 minutes |
| 99.999% | five nines | 5.26 minutes | 26 seconds |

Two nines sounds fine — until you picture a payment processor or hospital records
system offline for three and a half days. Five nines means the system can only
blink for about **5 minutes across an entire year**, which leaves no room for a
human to even notice, let alone react.

<Callout type="tip">
The right target depends on the stakes. A side project is fine at **99%**. A
consumer e-commerce site aims for **99.9%**. A bank or air-traffic system needs
**99.999%**. Every extra nine costs real money — redundant hardware across data
centers, deep monitoring, and automated failover — so buy only the nines you need.
</Callout>

## Reliability: being *right*, not just *up*

**Reliability** is related to availability but distinct. An **available** system
is reachable. A **reliable** system also returns **correct** results.

- A database that is up but returns corrupted data is available, not reliable.
- A search engine that responds fast but with irrelevant results is available, not reliable.

Reliability is harder to measure because "correct" is **domain-specific**:

- **Search** → returning relevant results.
- **Payments** → money debited from exactly one account and credited to exactly one other.
- **Messaging** → messages delivered at least once, exactly once, or in order.

Defining what "correct" means for *your* system is the prerequisite for measuring
reliability — it forces you to be precise about the promises you make. Getting there
takes both **preventing** errors (input validation, idempotent operations, testing)
and **handling** them gracefully when they slip through (designing for partial failure).

## Fault tolerance

**Fault tolerance** is a system's ability to keep working **correctly even when
some components fail**. Hardware dies, networks partition, software has bugs — a
fault-tolerant system expects all of this and survives it. Note the difference:
fault tolerance is about *surviving* faults, not *preventing* them.

**Redundancy** is the foundational technique. Two service instances mean one can
crash while the other serves. Data on three disks survives one disk dying. But
redundancy adds hard questions: Which copy is authoritative? How do you detect a
failure? How do you recover? (These lead straight into consensus, leader election,
and health checking.)

A **single point of failure (SPOF)** is any component whose failure takes down the
whole system. Hunting down and removing SPOFs is *the* core activity of building
highly available systems. Common ones:

- A single load balancer
- A single database with no replicas
- A single DNS server or network switch
- A single auth service with no fallback
- A third-party API you depend on with no backup path

<Callout type="tip">
Redundancy only helps if the copies fail **independently**. Two servers in the same
rack share one power strip and one switch — one tripped breaker takes out "both."
Real high availability spreads copies across racks, zones, and regions so a single
event can't hit them all.
</Callout>

## High availability patterns

| Pattern | How it works | Pro | Con |
|---|---|---|---|
| **Active-passive** | One instance serves; standby idles and replicates state | Simple to reason about | Standby is wasted capacity |
| **Active-active** | All instances serve live traffic | Efficient, lower latency | State sync + conflicting writes are hard |

**Active-passive** (primary-standby) is the simplest pattern: one instance handles
everything while standbys sit idle, ready to take over on failover. Easy to reason
about, but the idle standby is paid-for capacity doing nothing.

**Active-active** spreads traffic across all instances at once; if one dies, its
load is redistributed. It uses resources better and eases geographic distribution.
The catch is state: stateless services (like most HTTP APIs) are natural fits, while
stateful services (like databases) need careful conflict handling.

The **health check** ties it all together. A load balancer probes each instance;
failing ones are pulled from rotation, recovered ones are added back. Designing them
is subtle — too lenient and you route traffic to broken instances; too aggressive and
you evict healthy instances during a brief slowdown, cutting capacity exactly when
you need it most.

## Where this shows up

- **Banks and stock exchanges** → five nines. A few minutes of downtime means lost
  money and regulatory trouble, so they run redundant everything across data centers.
- **Consumer e-commerce** → ~99.9%. Occasional blips are survivable, but checkout
  outages during a sale are brutal — hence active-active fleets behind load balancers.
- **Streaming and social feeds** → high availability but relaxed reliability. Showing
  a slightly stale feed beats showing an error page; correctness bends for uptime.
- **Payment ledgers** → reliability trumps availability. Better to reject a transaction
  than to double-charge; "correct or nothing" wins over "always answer."
- **Cloud object storage (e.g. S3)** → sells *durability* (11 nines) separately from
  *availability* — your data is safe even during a brief outage window.

<Callout type="warning">
The trap: **chasing more nines without killing your SPOFs.** You can buy expensive
five-nines hardware and still get taken down by one shared load balancer, one DNS
provider, or one un-replicated database. Availability is capped by your *weakest*
dependency, not your strongest. Map every dependency — including third parties — and
ask "what happens when this one dies?" A second trap is a **health check that always
passes**, which happily routes users to broken instances while your dashboard stays green.
</Callout>

## Handling the pressure points

- **Hidden SPOF?** → draw the full dependency graph and add redundancy or a fallback
  for anything with only one copy, including external APIs.
- **Correlated failures?** → spread replicas across zones/regions so one event can't
  take them all out at once.
- **Bad health checks?** → probe a real code path (hit the DB, not just `/ping`), and
  tune thresholds so transient blips don't evict healthy nodes.
- **Failover that never gets tested?** → run game days and chaos experiments; an
  untested failover is a SPOF wearing a disguise.

## SLAs, SLOs, and SLIs

These three are constantly confused, but they form a clean hierarchy — from raw
measurement, to internal target, to customer promise.

| Term | What it is | Example | Consequence if missed |
|---|---|---|---|
| **SLI** (Indicator) | The actual measurement | p99 latency = 240ms | None — it's just data |
| **SLO** (Objective) | Your internal target | "p99 latency < 300ms" | Internal signal to fix things |
| **SLA** (Agreement) | Contractual promise to customers | "99.5% uptime guaranteed" | Refunds / penalties |

- **SLIs** are objective measurements of what your system is actually doing:
  error rate, p99 latency, or data durability.
- **SLOs** are the targets you set for those SLIs. Meet them and users are probably
  happy; miss them often and users are probably suffering.
- **SLAs** are contracts. They're usually *looser* than your SLOs on purpose — your
  internal SLO might be 99.9% while the SLA promises 99.5%, giving you a buffer before
  you owe refunds.

<Callout type="tip">
**Error budgets** make this concrete. A 99.9% SLO gives you a **0.1% budget** — about
**43 minutes** of downtime in a 30-day month. Spend it on planned maintenance, risky
deploys, and incidents. When it runs out, that's the signal to freeze risky releases
and pour effort into stability until the budget recovers.
</Callout>

## Alternatives & related ideas

<Callout type="alternative">
When perfect uptime is too costly, you **degrade instead of dying**. **Graceful
degradation** serves a reduced experience (cached feed, "add to cart later") rather
than an error. **Circuit breakers** stop hammering a failing dependency and fall back
fast. **Load shedding** drops low-priority requests to keep the core alive under
overload. And for reliability specifically, **idempotency** and **retries with
backoff** let clients safely re-try without double-charging — buying correctness even
when the network misbehaves.
</Callout>

## Summary

**Availability** is how often your system is up, measured in **nines** — each extra
nine cuts allowed downtime tenfold, and each one costs real money. **Reliability** is
a different promise: returning *correct* results, not just being reachable. You reach
both by killing **single points of failure** with redundancy that fails independently,
choosing active-passive or active-active based on state, and writing health checks
that test real behavior. Finally, **SLIs, SLOs, and SLAs** turn quality into numbers,
and **error budgets** turn reliability into a resource you deliberately spend on
shipping — buy only the nines your users actually need.

<Callout type="analogy">
System design interviews are like a driving test. You don't fail because you
couldn't parallel-park a semi-truck through a keyhole — you fail on the
**avoidable stuff**: you didn't check your mirrors, you rolled through a stop
sign, you never signaled.

The hard maneuvers are rarely what sink candidates. The **avoidable mistakes**
are. Master those, and you clear the bar before you've drawn a single clever
diagram.
</Callout>

Most candidates don't fail because they lack knowledge. They fail because they
make a small number of **preventable process mistakes** under pressure. Learn to
spot and avoid these, and you'll outperform people who know more than you.

## The mistakes at a glance

| Mistake | What it signals | Do this instead |
|---|---|---|
| **Jumping to a solution** | You solve the wrong problem | Clarify requirements first |
| **Skipping scale estimation** | You've never sized production | Do 5 minutes of back-of-envelope math |
| **Ignoring failure modes** | You've only built prototypes | Ask "what happens when this fails?" |
| **No trade-off discussion** | Shallow depth | Say "X for Y reason, trade-off Z" |
| **Silence** | You're stuck (even if you aren't) | Narrate your thinking out loud |
| **Over-engineering** | Poor judgment about scope | Match complexity to the requirements |

## Mistake 1: Jumping to the solution

You hear "design a URL shortener" and immediately start drawing boxes — web
server, database, cache — before understanding anything about the requirements.

The trap: a shortener for a personal tool and one serving **billions of
redirects a day** are completely different designs. Guess wrong and you've
burned the first 15 minutes of a 45-minute interview solving the wrong problem.

<Callout type="warning">
**Design before you understand and you design the wrong system.** Before you
touch the whiteboard, spend five minutes on questions: What are the core
features? What scale? What are the latency and availability targets?

Asking is **not** weakness — it's discipline. Senior engineers don't design
before they understand.
</Callout>

<Callout type="tip">
After clarifying, **state your understanding back**: "I'll design for 300M DAU,
write latency under 500ms, read under 50ms. Core features: shortening and click
tracking. Out of scope: auth and analytics dashboards. Sound right?" This locks
in alignment and lets the interviewer redirect you before you invest time.
</Callout>

## Mistake 2: Skipping scale estimation

The second trap is jumping straight from requirements to design without
estimating what the system must handle. That produces designs that are either
badly **under-built** (one database for something that needs sharding) or
badly **over-built** (a distributed queue for 100 requests a day).

Back-of-the-envelope math isn't busywork — it's what decides whether you need:

- a **cache** (read QPS)
- **replication** (availability + read load)
- **sharding** (data won't fit on one server)
- a **CDN** (bandwidth exceeds normal links)

<Callout type="tip">
Spend five minutes. Compute **QPS** (`DAU × actions/day ÷ 86400`), **peak QPS**
(~2× average), storage per year, and bandwidth. Round aggressively — you want
order of magnitude, not precision. Then *use* the numbers: "At 680 peak read
QPS, one replica plus a modest cache handles the read path — no distributed read
tier needed yet."
</Callout>

## Mistake 3: Ignoring failure modes

A system that only works when every component is healthy is a **prototype, not a
production system**. Real systems fail constantly: disks fill, connections
exhaust, dependencies time out, hardware dies at 3am.

<Callout type="warning">
**Ignoring failure signals you've never operated at scale.** You don't need to
solve every failure — you need to show you see the major ones. Walk the system
and ask "what happens when *this* fails?" for each component:

- Primary database dies → is there a replica? What's the failover window?
- Cache goes cold after a deploy → can the database absorb the full load?
- Message queue stops accepting writes → does the write path block or degrade?
</Callout>

One sentence is enough to earn the point: "I'd make the cache layer a replicated
Redis cluster with automatic failover, and ensure the app tolerates cache misses
without falling over."

## Mistake 4: Not discussing trade-offs

Asserting choices without naming alternatives signals **shallow depth**. "I'll
use NoSQL" means nothing on its own. "I'll use NoSQL **because** our access is
always by primary key and we need horizontal write scaling — the **trade-off** is
we lose complex multi-table queries, which is acceptable here" shows judgment.

<Callout type="warning">
**Interviewers aren't grading the answer — they're grading your reasoning.**
They want evidence you know why each option exists and what it costs. If you make
a bare assertion, they can't tell whether you understand the choice or just
memorized it.

Use the formula on every significant decision: **"X for Y reason, with the
trade-off that Z."** Database, consistency model, caching strategy, API style.
</Callout>

## Mistake 5: Silence

Thinking in silence is **invisible** to the interviewer. Stare at the whiteboard
with the marker uncapped for 45 seconds and they can't tell whether you're about
to have a brilliant insight or you're completely stuck. From their seat, silence
looks like stuck.

The fix: **narrate in real time.** "I'm choosing between pull-based and
push-based fan-out for the timeline. Pull is simpler — the client fetches from
followed accounts on open — but it hits thousands of follow relationships per
request. Push precomputes timelines on write: fast reads, but expensive for
users with millions of followers." Now they're watching you reason — exactly
what they want.

<Callout type="tip">
Narrating helps *you* too — a half-formed idea sharpens the moment you say it out
loud. And if you're genuinely stuck, **say so and ask**: "I'm unsure about cache
invalidation here — any direction you'd like me to explore?" That reads as
honesty and collaboration, not weakness.
</Callout>

## Mistake 6: Over-engineering

Adding unnecessary complexity is as harmful as missing necessary complexity.
Reaching for Kubernetes, an ML ranking model, a blockchain audit log, and a saga
pattern for a system serving **500 users** signals you optimize for looking
impressive over solving the actual problem.

<Callout type="warning">
**Over-engineering has real costs** — more infrastructure to run, more failure
modes, more code to maintain — and in an interview it burns the minutes you need
for the parts that matter. Follow **YAGNI** (You Aren't Gonna Need It): a
simpler solution that fits the requirements beats an elaborate one that solves a
problem you don't have.

Interviewers grade your judgment about *what to build*, not just what you know.
Straightforward solutions for straightforward problems demonstrate that judgment.
</Callout>

## Bonus tips for interview readiness

Beyond avoiding the six, a few habits separate polished candidates:

- **Practice drawing quickly.** A clean, legible diagram the interviewer can
  follow in real time makes your design far easier to evaluate. Drill your boxes,
  arrows, and labels until they're fast and habitual.
- **Go deep on 3–5 systems.** Real depth on a handful beats shallow knowledge of
  many. Know a URL shortener, a real-time chat app, and a distributed rate
  limiter end-to-end — that gives you a vocabulary of patterns to recombine.
- **Read real engineering blogs.** Uber, Discord, and Netflix publish how they
  solved real problems at real scale. A post on how Discord scaled from 100K to
  1M concurrent users teaches more about actual trade-offs than any textbook.

## Summary

You rarely fail a system design interview for lacking exotic knowledge — you
fail on **avoidable process mistakes**. **Clarify requirements** before you
design, **estimate scale** before you commit to an architecture, and treat
**failure modes** as first-class. On every significant choice, name the
**trade-off** ("X for Y, giving up Z"); **narrate out loud** so your reasoning is
visible; and **match complexity to the requirements** instead of reaching for
impressive-sounding machinery. Nail these fundamentals and you'll clear the bar
before you draw a single clever diagram.

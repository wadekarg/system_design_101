## The Framework

A system design interview typically runs 45 to 60 minutes. In that window, you must convince an experienced engineer that you can take an ambiguous, open-ended problem and turn it into a concrete, reasoned architecture. Most candidates fail not because they lack technical knowledge, but because they jump straight into drawing boxes without stopping to understand what they are actually building.

The five-step framework below gives you a repeatable structure. It ensures you spend time where it matters, signal the right skills at the right moments, and avoid the most common interview anti-patterns. Practice it until it becomes muscle memory, so that in a high-pressure interview you can focus your mental energy on the technical substance rather than on managing the conversation.

The five steps, with rough time allocations for a 45-minute interview:

1. Clarify Requirements — 5 minutes
2. Estimate Scale — 5 minutes
3. High-Level Design — 15 minutes
4. Deep Dive — 15 minutes
5. Identify Bottlenecks and Trade-offs — 5 minutes

## Step 1: Clarify Requirements (5 min)

Before touching the whiteboard or diagramming a single component, spend the first five minutes asking questions. This is the most important step, and the one most candidates skip in their anxiety to appear decisive.

There are two categories of requirements to nail down.

**Functional requirements** define what the system must do. Ask: What are the core features? Which are in scope for this interview and which are explicitly out of scope? If the prompt is "design Twitter," clarify whether you need to handle direct messages, trends, search, or just the core tweet/timeline flow. Getting this wrong means designing a system for a different problem than the interviewer intended.

**Non-functional requirements** define how well the system must do it. Ask: How many users are we targeting — thousands, millions, hundreds of millions? What is the acceptable read latency? Does the system need to be strongly consistent, or is eventual consistency acceptable? Is this read-heavy or write-heavy? Are there regulatory constraints around data residency or retention?

Finally, clarify **constraints**. Is this a greenfield design or are there existing systems to integrate with? Are there technology choices already made?

This step does three things simultaneously: it ensures you build the right system, it signals that you think before you act, and it gives you the inputs you need for estimation in Step 2. An interviewer who sees you ask sharp clarifying questions already has a positive impression before you draw a single box.

## Step 2: Estimate Scale (5 min)

Scale estimation — "back of the envelope" — is a filter for design decisions. The database choice, the caching strategy, the replication topology — all of these depend on whether you are handling 1,000 requests per second or 1,000,000. Guessing without estimating is like choosing between walking and flying a plane based on where you're going without knowing whether the destination is 2 km or 2,000 km away.

You do not need precision. You need the right order of magnitude. Estimate:

- **DAU (Daily Active Users):** how many people use the system on a typical day?
- **QPS (Queries per Second):** `QPS = DAU × (actions per user per day) / 86400`
- **Peak QPS:** roughly 2x the average, to account for traffic spikes
- **Storage:** how much data is written per second, and for how long must it be retained?
- **Bandwidth:** how much data moves in and out per second?

Round aggressively. `86,400` seconds in a day is close enough to `100,000` for this purpose. If 300 million users each post once a day on average, that is 3 million posts per day, roughly 35 writes per second, and a peak of about 70 writes per second. Those numbers tell you this is not a write-heavy system at the database level — you can design accordingly.

Doing this step signals seniority. Junior engineers skip it and end up defending a design that would either collapse at the stated scale or be embarrassingly over-engineered for the actual load.

## Step 3: High-Level Design (15 min)

Now you draw. The goal at this stage is to get a working, correct skeleton on paper — not a detailed one. Start with the major components and the data flow between them.

Draw boxes for the obvious actors and services: clients, load balancer, application servers, primary database, cache, object storage if relevant, message queue if relevant. Then draw arrows showing how data moves. A user posts a tweet: where does the request land first? How does it reach the database? How does another user's timeline read eventually reflect that tweet?

Stay at a high level of abstraction. Do not choose a specific database product yet. Do not specify cache eviction policies. Do not define API schemas. The purpose of this phase is to ensure the system is logically correct — that you have identified all the necessary pieces and they are connected sensibly. A design that is logically incomplete at the high level cannot be saved by clever details later.

Walk the interviewer through your diagram as you draw it. Narrate the happy path first: what happens when everything works. Then briefly note where the critical data boundaries are — where does the system cross a process boundary, a network boundary, a consistency boundary.

This is also where you make a first pass at your data model. What are the primary entities? What are the access patterns? Knowing whether you primarily query by user ID, by time, or by both will influence your storage choices in the deep dive.

## Step 4: Deep Dive (15 min)

After the high-level sketch, the interviewer will typically steer you toward one or two specific components for a deeper examination. Common deep-dive targets include: the database schema and choice, the caching layer, the API design, and any asynchronous processing or messaging system.

Be prepared to go deep on any of the following areas:

**Database choice:** Relational databases offer ACID transactions, structured schemas, and complex queries via SQL. NoSQL databases offer flexible schemas, horizontal scale-out, and tuning for specific access patterns. The choice depends on your data model, your consistency requirements, and your scale. Be ready to defend whichever you chose, including acknowledging what you give up.

**Caching strategy:** What do you cache? How long? Where does the cache live — in-process, dedicated cache server, or CDN? What happens on a cache miss? What is your invalidation strategy when the underlying data changes? These are not hypothetical questions; they are decisions that make or break production systems.

**API design:** Define the key endpoints. What are the request and response shapes? How do you handle pagination for large result sets? What authentication scheme do you use? How do you version the API?

Answer the deep dive questions with the same structure you used in Step 1: state your requirement, explain your choice, and name the trade-off you are accepting.

## Step 5: Identify Bottlenecks and Trade-offs (5 min)

In the final minutes, turn a critical eye on your own design. Every system has bottlenecks — places where load concentrates and which will fail first. Finding yours before the interviewer does is a sign of experience.

Walk through the system looking for single points of failure: components that have no redundancy and whose failure would take the system down. The primary database is the classic example. Your design should address how you handle its failure — through replication, failover, or another mechanism.

Ask yourself what happens at 10x the scale you designed for. Which component becomes the bottleneck first? The database? The application server? The network? How would you address it?

Identify the places where you made trade-offs. "I chose async replication for low write latency, accepting that reads might see slightly stale data." "I use a cache with a 5-minute TTL, which means a user might see a profile update after up to 5 minutes of delay." Naming your trade-offs shows you understand the system's actual behavior, not just its happy path.

## Common Mistakes

A few pitfalls to avoid throughout the interview:

- **Jumping to solutions:** The single most common mistake. Discipline yourself to complete Step 1 fully before drawing anything.
- **Silence:** Thinking in silence for 30 seconds looks like being stuck. Narrate your reasoning: "I'm considering whether to use a relational or NoSQL database here — let me think through the access patterns."
- **Whiteboard neglect:** Draw as you talk. A messy evolving diagram with clear labels communicates more than a perfect mental model the interviewer cannot see.
- **Asserting without reasoning:** "I'll use Cassandra" means nothing without "because we need geographic replication and our access pattern is always by partition key, which makes Cassandra's data model a natural fit."
- **Skipping failure modes:** A system that only works when everything is healthy is not a production system. Always discuss at least one failure scenario.

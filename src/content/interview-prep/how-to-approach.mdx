<Callout type="analogy">
Think of yourself as an **architect meeting a client** for the first time. A good architect doesn't grab a pencil and start sketching a house the moment the client sits down. They ask: How many people will live here? What's the budget? Do you need a garage, a home office, room to expand later?

Only after the requirements are clear do they draw — first a rough floor plan, then the details of the kitchen the client cares most about. A system design interview is the same: **clarify before you draw.** The candidates who fail almost always start sketching too soon.
</Callout>

## The framework at a glance

A system design interview typically runs **45 to 60 minutes**. In that window you must convince an experienced engineer that you can take an ambiguous, open-ended problem and turn it into a concrete, reasoned architecture.

Most candidates fail not because they lack technical knowledge, but because they **jump straight into drawing boxes** without stopping to understand what they're actually building.

The five phases below give you a repeatable structure. Practice them until they're muscle memory, so in the interview your energy goes to the technical substance — not to managing the conversation.

| Phase | What you do | Time (45-min) | What it signals |
|---|---|---|---|
| 1. Clarify requirements | Ask functional + non-functional questions | ~5 min | You think before you act |
| 2. Estimate scale | Back-of-envelope QPS, storage, bandwidth | ~5 min | You reason about scale |
| 3. High-level design | Draw components and data flow | ~15 min | You can architect a whole system |
| 4. Deep dive | Detail 1–2 components the interviewer picks | ~15 min | You have real depth |
| 5. Bottlenecks & trade-offs | Critique your own design | ~5 min | You know how systems actually fail |

## Phase 1: Clarify requirements (~5 min)

Before touching the whiteboard, spend the first five minutes **asking questions**. This is the most important phase, and the one most candidates skip in their rush to look decisive.

There are two categories to nail down:

- **Functional requirements** — *what* the system must do. What are the core features? Which are in scope, which are explicitly out? For "design Twitter," clarify whether you handle DMs, trends, and search — or just the core tweet/timeline flow.
- **Non-functional requirements** — *how well* it must do it. How many users: thousands, millions, hundreds of millions? Acceptable read latency? Strong or eventual consistency? Read-heavy or write-heavy? Any data-residency or retention rules?

Then clarify **constraints**: greenfield or integrating with existing systems? Any technology choices already locked in?

This phase does three things at once: it ensures you build the *right* system, it signals that you think before acting, and it gives you the inputs for estimation next.

<Callout type="tip">
In practice: keep a mental checklist — **scope, scale, consistency, read/write ratio, constraints**. An interviewer who watches you ask sharp questions already has a positive impression before you draw a single box.
</Callout>

## Phase 2: Estimate scale (~5 min)

Scale estimation — "back of the envelope" — is a **filter for design decisions**. Your database choice, caching strategy, and replication topology all depend on whether you're handling 1,000 requests per second or 1,000,000.

You don't need precision. You need the right **order of magnitude**. Estimate:

- **DAU (Daily Active Users)** — how many people use it on a typical day?
- **QPS (Queries per Second)** — `QPS = DAU × (actions per user per day) / 86,400`
- **Peak QPS** — roughly 2× the average, to cover traffic spikes
- **Storage** — data written per second × how long you retain it
- **Bandwidth** — data in and out per second

Round aggressively. `86,400` seconds/day is close enough to `100,000`. If 300M users each post once daily, that's ~3M posts/day ≈ **35 writes/sec**, peaking near **70 writes/sec**. That tells you this *isn't* a write-heavy system at the database level — so you can design accordingly.

<Callout type="tip">
In practice: doing this phase signals seniority. Junior engineers skip it and end up defending a design that would either collapse at the stated scale or be wildly over-engineered for the actual load.
</Callout>

## Phase 3: High-level design (~15 min)

Now you draw. The goal is a **working, correct skeleton** — not a detailed one. Start with the major components and the data flow between them.

Draw boxes for the obvious actors: clients, load balancer, application servers, primary database, cache, and — if relevant — object storage and a message queue. Then draw arrows showing how data moves. A user posts a tweet: where does the request land first? How does it reach the database? How does another user's timeline eventually reflect it?

**Stay abstract.** Don't pick a specific database product, cache eviction policy, or API schema yet. This phase proves the system is *logically correct* — all the pieces exist and connect sensibly. A design that's logically incomplete here can't be rescued by clever details later.

Walk the interviewer through the diagram as you draw. Narrate the **happy path** first, then note the critical boundaries — where the system crosses a process, network, or consistency boundary. This is also where you make a first pass at your **data model**: primary entities and access patterns. Knowing whether you query by user ID, by time, or both will shape your storage choices in the deep dive.

## Phase 4: Deep dive (~15 min)

After the sketch, the interviewer steers you toward **one or two components** for a closer look. Be ready to go deep on any of these:

- **Database choice** — Relational gives you ACID transactions, structured schemas, and rich SQL queries. NoSQL gives you flexible schemas, horizontal scale-out, and tuning for specific access patterns. Defend whichever you chose *and* name what you give up.
- **Caching strategy** — What do you cache, for how long, and where (in-process, dedicated server, or CDN)? What happens on a miss? What's your invalidation strategy when the underlying data changes?
- **API design** — Key endpoints, request/response shapes, pagination for large results, authentication, and versioning.

Answer deep-dive questions with the same structure every time: **state the requirement, explain your choice, name the trade-off** you're accepting.

## Phase 5: Identify bottlenecks and trade-offs (~5 min)

In the final minutes, turn a critical eye on your **own** design. Finding your weak spots before the interviewer does is a mark of experience.

- **Single points of failure** — components with no redundancy whose failure takes the system down. The primary database is the classic one; say how you'd handle it (replication, failover).
- **Behavior at 10× scale** — which component breaks first? The database, the app tier, the network? How would you address it?
- **Your trade-offs** — name them explicitly: *"I chose async replication for low write latency, accepting that reads may see slightly stale data."* Naming trade-offs shows you understand the system's real behavior, not just its happy path.

<Callout type="warning">
The single biggest mistake: **jumping straight to the solution** — drawing boxes or blurting "I'll use Cassandra" before you've clarified requirements. It makes you look like you can code but can't *design*, and you often end up building the wrong system entirely.

How to avoid it: force yourself to finish Phase 1 before your pen touches the board. If you catch yourself designing early, stop and ask one more clarifying question. The discipline is the signal.
</Callout>

## Other common mistakes to avoid

- **Silence** — thinking quietly for 30 seconds looks like being stuck. Narrate: *"I'm weighing relational vs NoSQL here — let me think through the access patterns."*
- **Whiteboard neglect** — draw as you talk. A messy, clearly-labeled, evolving diagram beats a perfect mental model the interviewer can't see.
- **Asserting without reasoning** — *"I'll use Cassandra"* means nothing without *"because we need geographic replication and always query by partition key."*
- **Skipping failure modes** — a system that only works when everything is healthy isn't a production system. Always discuss at least one failure scenario.

## Summary

Treat the interview like an architect meeting a client: **clarify before you draw.** Move through five phases on a rough time budget — clarify requirements (~5 min), estimate scale (~5 min), high-level design (~15 min), deep dive (~15 min), and bottlenecks & trade-offs (~5 min). Stay abstract early and detailed late, and let the interviewer steer the deep dive. Above all, **narrate your reasoning out loud** and never jump to a solution before you understand the problem — the process is what's being scored, not just the final diagram.

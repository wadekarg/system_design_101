<Callout type="analogy">

Think of the old project-management joke: **"fast, cheap, good — pick two."**
You can't have all three, and pretending you can is how projects fail.

System design works the same way. Every choice sits on a see-saw: push one side
down (more consistency, more speed, more simplicity) and another side rises (less
availability, less throughput, less flexibility). Your job isn't to find the
magic option with no downside — it doesn't exist. Your job is to know **which
side you're pushing down, and why that's the right call here.**

</Callout>

## Why trade-offs matter

In a university course, problems have correct answers. In system design, they
have **contextually appropriate answers** — and every appropriate answer comes
with something you give up.

There is no database that is always right. No architecture that is always the
right shape. No consistency model that is always the right behavior. This isn't a
weakness of the field — it's a property of building real systems under real
constraints:

- **Physics** — speed of light, disk seek times, network latency
- **Economics** — cost of servers, cost of engineering time
- **Product** — latency targets, consistency needs, budget

Optimizing one dimension almost always costs you another. Senior engineers stand
out not by knowing the "best" choice in the abstract, but by understanding what
each choice **buys** and what it **costs** in a specific context — and saying so
clearly.

<Callout type="warning">

The biggest mistake candidates make: **treating a choice as free.** Saying
"I'll use a cache" or "I'll add replicas" as if it only has upside is a red flag
— caches go stale, replicas lag, queues add latency, microservices add
operational pain.

**How to avoid it:** every time you pick something, finish the sentence out loud
— "...and the cost of that is ___." If you can't name the cost, you don't yet
understand the decision, and the interviewer will notice.

</Callout>

## Consistency vs Availability (CAP)

The **CAP theorem** (Eric Brewer) says a distributed system can guarantee at most
**two of three** properties at once:

- **Consistency (C)** — every read gets the most recent write, or an error
- **Availability (A)** — every request gets a response (maybe not the latest data)
- **Partition tolerance (P)** — the system keeps working even when messages
  between nodes are dropped or delayed

The catch: in any real distributed system, **partitions will happen** — the
network is unreliable, so you can't opt out of P. That means the real choice is
always **CP vs AP**: when a partition hits, do you stay *consistent* (and maybe
refuse requests) or stay *available* (and maybe serve stale data)?

| Model | Sacrifices | Examples | Good for |
|---|---|---|---|
| **CA** (single node) | Partition tolerance | Single-node PostgreSQL | Transactional work you fully control |
| **CP** | Availability | ZooKeeper, HBase | Coordination, locks, correctness-critical data |
| **AP** | Consistency | Cassandra, DynamoDB | Carts, feeds, preferences, sensor data |

<Callout type="tip">

**In practice:** ZooKeeper stops serving if a majority of nodes are unreachable —
handing out wrong coordination data (two "leaders", a double-held lock) is far
worse than a brief outage, so it chooses CP. A product-recommendation service
showing last week's "popular items" during a partition happily chooses AP.

</Callout>

Knowing where your system sits in the CAP triangle is often the **first step** in
picking storage. A payment system that must never double-charge leans **CP**. A
feed that can show slightly stale posts leans **AP**.

## Latency vs Consistency

CAP is the high-level frame. Latency-vs-consistency is where it plays out in the
replication details.

| | **Synchronous replication** | **Asynchronous replication** |
|---|---|---|
| When it acks | After all replicas have the write | As soon as the primary commits |
| Latency | Higher (waits for slowest replica) | Lower |
| Data-loss risk | None once acked | Writes in the lag window can be lost |
| Fits | Financial records, orders | Social feeds, real-time games |

The choice is driven by the **product requirement**, not technology taste. Money
that can't be lost demands **synchronous**. A multiplayer game where a 200ms ack
is worse than an occasional lost event might pick **asynchronous**.

<Callout type="tip">

**In practice:** many systems make this tunable per write. Cassandra's consistency
levels let you say `QUORUM` (a majority must respond) for important writes and
`ONE` (fastest available replica) for cheap ones — so you're not forced into one
setting for the whole app.

</Callout>

## SQL vs NoSQL

Often framed as old-vs-new, this is really a trade-off between properties
different workloads value differently.

| | **SQL (relational)** | **NoSQL** |
|---|---|---|
| Guarantees | ACID, enforced schema | Often relaxed / eventual |
| Queries | Joins, aggregations, arbitrary filters | Optimized for known access patterns |
| Schema | Fixed | Flexible / evolving |
| Scaling | Usually vertical | Horizontal across many nodes |
| Examples | PostgreSQL, MySQL | MongoDB, Cassandra, DynamoDB, Redis |

- **Choose SQL when** the data is relational, you need strong transactions, your
  access patterns are varied and unpredictable, or the data fits on one strong server.
- **Choose NoSQL when** the data is a poor fit for tables (documents, graphs),
  access is simple and predictable (lookup by key), or you need horizontal **write**
  scale or a rapidly evolving schema.

<Callout type="tip">

**In practice — the default:** start with **SQL**. It covers most use cases, the
tooling is mature, and the semantics are well understood. Reach for NoSQL when you
have a concrete reason — usually horizontal write scale or a document/graph model
that's genuinely awkward in tables.

</Callout>

## Normalization vs Denormalization

A trade-off that lives *inside* your database schema.

| | **Normalized** | **Denormalized** |
|---|---|---|
| Data copies | One source of truth, split across tables | Duplicated / pre-joined |
| Writes | Simple, no duplicates to update | Must update every copy |
| Reads | Need joins (slower) | Fast, data is pre-assembled |
| Best for | Write-heavy, integrity-critical | Read-heavy, latency-critical |

Normalize to keep data consistent and writes cheap; denormalize to make reads
fast by trading storage and write complexity. Feeds, timelines, and analytics
dashboards often denormalize hard so a read is a single lookup.

## Push vs Pull

How does data get to a consumer — does the source **push** it, or does the
consumer **pull** it?

| | **Push** | **Pull** |
|---|---|---|
| Trigger | Source sends on change | Consumer asks on a schedule |
| Freshness | Real-time | As fresh as the poll interval |
| Load pattern | Spikes when many update at once | Steady, predictable |
| Waste | Little | Polls that return nothing |
| Examples | WebSockets, webhooks, notifications | Cron jobs, feed refresh, monitoring |

**Push** wins when freshness matters and updates are sparse (chat, alerts).
**Pull** wins when you want simple, predictable load and can tolerate a little
staleness (a dashboard refreshing every 30s). Hybrids (long-polling,
push-then-pull-the-detail) borrow from both.

## Monolith vs Microservices

An architectural trade-off that plays out over a system's lifetime.

- **Monolith** — one codebase, one deploy, one log stream. Easy to run, debug,
  and reason about; transactions span the whole app. But it gets harder to scale
  as the team grows, a small change redeploys everything, and one slow feature can
  drag down the rest.
- **Microservices** — independently deployable services, each owning its data and
  API. Teams ship in parallel; a hot service scales alone. The cost is **operational
  complexity**: network calls that fail or time out, distributed tracing, and data
  that used to be one SQL join now spread across services.

<Callout type="tip">

**In practice:** most teams should **not** start with microservices. Build a
well-structured monolith with clear module boundaries first. When a concrete
problem appears — one feature too slow to deploy on its own, one component needing
independent scale — extract *that* piece into a service. Microservices solve
specific organizational and scaling problems; they aren't a default.

</Callout>

## How to frame trade-offs in an interview

The goal is not the "right" answer — it's to show you understand the trade-off and
are choosing deliberately for the requirements at hand.

The most effective template:

> **"I would choose X because Y, with the trade-off that Z."**

For example: *"I'd use asynchronous replication here because our latency target is
under 100ms and a social feed can tolerate slightly stale data — a post appearing
a few seconds late is fine. The trade-off is that if the primary fails during the
replication lag window, we could lose a few seconds of recent writes."*

That single sentence shows three things: you know the **options**, you know the
relevant **requirement**, and you know what you're **giving up**.

Never assert a choice without naming the alternative and why you passed on it.
"I'll use DynamoDB" tells the interviewer nothing. "I'll use DynamoDB because our
access pattern is always by user ID and we need horizontal write scale across
regions — we don't need complex queries, so we're fine giving up SQL's
flexibility" tells them you think in trade-offs, not checkboxes.

## Summary

Every system design decision buys something and costs something — the skill is
naming both out loud. Because network partitions are unavoidable, distributed
systems really choose **CP vs AP**, which shows up in the field as latency-vs-
consistency (sync vs async replication). The same shape repeats everywhere: **SQL
vs NoSQL**, **normalize vs denormalize**, **push vs pull**, **monolith vs
microservices** — each trades one property for another, and the "right" pick
depends entirely on your requirements. Treat no choice as free, and frame every
decision as **"I choose X because Y, accepting the trade-off of Z."** That
reasoning, not a memorized winner, is what interviewers are listening for.
